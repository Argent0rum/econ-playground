IS–LM: fiscal and monetary policy

IS–LM puts the goods market and the money market on one graph. It shows why a fiscal stimulus raises interest rates, and why monetary policy lowers them.

The idea

The IS curve shows combinations of the interest rate and output where the goods market is in equilibrium. It slopes down: a lower interest rate makes borrowing cheaper, investment rises, and through the multiplier output rises. Fiscal policy (government spending, taxes) shifts IS.

The LM curve shows where the money market is in equilibrium: the demand for money (which rises with income and falls with the interest rate) equals the supply set by the central bank. It slopes up: higher income means people want more money for transactions, which pushes the interest rate up. Monetary policy shifts LM.

Where they cross, both markets clear. A fiscal expansion moves IS right and raises both output and the interest rate. The higher rate discourages private investment — crowding out — so the effect on output is smaller than the simple multiplier suggests. A monetary expansion moves LM right, lowering the rate and raising output.

A practical note for Latvia: as a euro area member, it has no monetary policy of its own — the European Central Bank sets interest rates for all members. So in a Latvian recession, fiscal policy is the main national tool. IS–LM is a simplified model (modern central banks set an interest rate rather than a money supply), but it's still the standard way to think about policy mixes.

Try this

  1. Click Fiscal stimulus and compare the two multipliers in the readout: that difference is crowding out.
  2. Click Both together. The interest rate barely moves but output jumps — policies that support each other.
  3. Raise the MPC and watch the IS curve flatten: the economy becomes more sensitive to interest rate changes.

Common mistakes

  • Moving along the curve when the policy should shift it: government spending shifts IS, the money supply shifts LM.
  • Forgetting that IS–LM holds the price level fixed — it's a short-run model.
