The Keynesian cross and the multiplier

When the government spends a euro, someone earns it — and spends part of that, which someone else earns and spends again. The multiplier adds up all those rounds.

The idea

In the short run, Keynesian economics says output is driven by aggregate expenditure: AE = C + I + G (+ net exports, left out here). Consumption depends partly on income: C = C₀ + MPC × Y, where the marginal propensity to consume (MPC) is the share of an extra euro of income that people spend.

Equilibrium is where planned spending equals output, so firms neither pile up unsold stock nor run out: the point where the AE line crosses the 45° line.

When spending rises by €1, output rises by more than €1. The first euro becomes someone's income; they spend MPC of it, which becomes someone else's income, and so on. Adding up 1 + MPC + MPC² + … gives the multiplier 1 / (1 − MPC). With an MPC of 0.75 that's 4.

Real-world multipliers are much smaller — often estimated between about 0.5 and 2 — because some of each round leaks into taxes, imports (very important for a small open economy like Latvia) and savings, and because higher interest rates can crowd out private investment (see IS–LM).

Common mistakes

  • Treating the simple multiplier as the real-world effect of a stimulus. Taxes, imports and interest rates make it much smaller.
  • Confusing the MPC (share of extra income spent) with the average share of income spent.

Key formulas

AE = C + I + G, C = C₀ + MPC × Y

Planned spending

Y* = (C₀ + I + G) / (1 − MPC)

Equilibrium output

Multiplier = 1 / (1 − MPC) = 1 / MPS

MPS = marginal propensity to save

With taxes and imports: 1 / (1 − MPC(1 − t) + MPM)
