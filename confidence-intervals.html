Samples and confidence intervals

We almost never see a whole population, only a sample. A confidence interval turns one sample into a range that, over many samples, catches the true value as often as promised.

Scenarios

The idea

Imagine you want the average monthly grocery spending of all students in Latvia. You can't ask everyone, so you ask a random sample of n students and compute their average. A different sample would give a different average — that variation is what statistics measures.

The spread of sample averages is the standard error, σ/√n. It shrinks with the square root of the sample size: four times as many people only halves the error. A confidence interval is the sample mean plus or minus a few standard errors: x̄ ± t* · s/√n.

The “95%” is a promise about the method, not about one interval. If you repeated the survey many times, about 95 out of 100 intervals would contain the true mean μ. Any single interval either contains μ or it doesn't — you just don't know which. The graph shows 40 surveys at once, so you can count the misses.

Higher confidence costs width: a 99% interval is wider than a 90% one. Bigger samples buy narrower intervals at the same confidence. With small samples we use the t distribution instead of the normal one, because the sample standard deviation s is itself uncertain.

Try this

  1. Press Draw 40 new samples a few times. At 95% confidence you'll usually see about 2 red intervals out of 40.
  2. Set n to 4. The intervals get much wider (and jumpy), but the share that misses stays about the same.
  3. Raise the confidence level to 99%. The misses almost disappear — but every interval gets wider.
  4. Move n from 20 to 80. The intervals become about half as wide: the √n rule.

Common mistakes

  • Saying “there is a 95% chance μ is in this interval”. After you've drawn the sample, μ is either in it or not; the 95% describes how often the method works.
  • Forgetting the square root. Doubling the sample does not halve the margin of error — you need four times the sample.
  • Using the normal value 1.96 with a tiny sample. With n under about 30, use the t value with n − 1 degrees of freedom.
