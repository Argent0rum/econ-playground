Costs and perfect competition

In a perfectly competitive market a firm can't choose its price — it takes the market price and only decides how much to produce. Its cost curves tell it the answer.

The idea

A firm in perfect competition is a price taker: there are so many sellers of an identical product that it can sell as much as it wants at the market price, and nothing at a higher price. So for this firm, price equals marginal revenue.

It maximises profit by producing where price = marginal cost. Below that quantity, one more unit adds more revenue than cost; above it, the extra unit costs more than it brings in.

Whether that output makes a profit depends on average total cost (ATC) at that quantity: profit = (P − ATC) × Q. If the price is between minimum average variable cost and minimum ATC, the firm loses money but still produces in the short run, because shutting down would mean losing all its fixed costs. Below minimum AVC (the shutdown point), it's better to stop.

In the long run, profits attract new firms and losses drive firms out, until price settles at minimum ATC — firms make zero economic profit, which still includes a normal return on the owner's money and time.

Common mistakes

  • Producing where profit per unit (P − ATC) is largest. The goal is the largest total profit, which is where P = MC.
  • Thinking a firm making a loss should always close. In the short run it compares price with average variable cost.
