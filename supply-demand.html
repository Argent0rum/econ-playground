Supply and demand

Prices come from two groups pulling in opposite directions: buyers who want more when it's cheap, and sellers who offer more when it pays well. Where they meet is the equilibrium.

The idea

The demand curve slopes down: when something gets cheaper, people buy more of it (and some new buyers appear). The supply curve slopes up: a higher price makes it worth producing more, and lets higher-cost producers join in.

The equilibrium is where the two cross. At any higher price, sellers would be left with unsold goods and would cut prices; at any lower price, buyers would compete for too few goods and bid the price up. The market drifts back to the crossing point.

It's crucial to separate a movement along a curve (the price changed) from a shift of the curve (something else changed). Demand shifts with income, tastes, prices of related goods, expectations and the number of buyers. Supply shifts with input costs, technology, taxes and subsidies, and the number of sellers.

The shaded areas measure who gains from trade. Consumer surplus is the gap between what buyers would have been willing to pay and what they actually pay. Producer surplus is the gap between the price and the lowest price sellers would have accepted. A free market maximises their sum; a binding price control shrinks it and creates a deadweight loss.

  1. Click Demand rises. Both price and quantity go up. Now try Supply falls — price goes up but quantity goes down. Same price change, opposite story.

  3. Set a ceiling above the equilibrium. Nothing happens: a control only matters if it binds.

Common mistakes

  • Saying “demand increased” when only the price fell. A lower price moves you along the demand curve; the curve itself didn't move.
  • Thinking a price ceiling helps all buyers. Those who get the good pay less, but some buyers can't get it at all.
