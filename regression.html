Correlation and least-squares regression

Regression draws the line that best fits a cloud of points and tells you how sure you can be about its slope. Here it estimates how much of each extra euro of income households spend.

The idea

Each dot is a household: monthly disposable income on the x-axis, consumption on the y-axis. Theory (the Keynesian consumption function, C = a + b·Y) says the slope b — the marginal propensity to consume — is between 0 and 1. Regression estimates it from data.

Ordinary least squares picks the line that makes the sum of squared residuals (the vertical gaps between dots and the line) as small as possible. The slope estimate b̂ always comes with a standard error: how much b̂ would bounce around if you drew another sample. Divide b̂ by its standard error and you get a t statistic; its p-value tests whether the true slope could be zero.

R² is the share of the variation in consumption that the line explains. A low R² does not mean the slope is wrong — consumption simply depends on many other things. The correlation coefficient r measures how tightly the dots follow a straight line (r² = R² in simple regression).

Least squares cares a lot about big errors, because they are squared. One unusual point far from the rest — an outlier with high leverage — can tilt the whole line.

Try this

  1. Press New sample several times. The blue fitted line moves around the purple true line — that wobble is what the standard error measures.
  2. Choose Noisy, small sample. The slope's confidence interval becomes very wide, and sometimes it isn't significant at all.
  3. Choose Weak relationship. R² is low, but with enough data the slope can still be clearly different from zero.
  4. Tick Add one outlier with a small sample and watch one point drag the line.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a low R² makes a regression useless. The slope can be estimated precisely even when income explains only a small part of spending.
  • Reading the slope as causal. Regression shows how y moves with x in the data; whether x causes y needs a design that rules out other explanations (see the next topic).
  • Extrapolating far outside the data. The line is only checked where you have observations.

Key formulas

b̂ = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) / Σ(x − x̄)²

Least-squares slope

â = ȳ − b̂ · x̄

Intercept

SE(b̂) = s / √Σ(x − x̄)²

Standard error of the slope, s² = Σe² / (n − 2)

t = b̂ / SE(b̂)

Test of H₀: b = 0, n − 2 degrees of freedom

R² = 1 − Σe² / Σ(y − ȳ)²
