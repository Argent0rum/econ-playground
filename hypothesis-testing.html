Hypothesis tests, p-values and power

A hypothesis test asks whether a result is too large to be just luck. The significance level, the sample size and the size of the real effect decide how often the test gets it right.

Scenarios

The idea

A university tries a tutoring programme: one group of n students gets tutoring, another doesn't, and we compare average exam scores. The null hypothesis H₀ says tutoring does nothing; any difference is luck. The alternative H₁ says it raises scores.

We turn the difference into a test statistic, t = difference ÷ standard error. If H₀ is true, t follows the purple curve centred on 0. We pick a significance level α (usually 5%) and reject H₀ when t lands in the orange tail — results that would happen less than α of the time by luck. The p-value is the probability of a result at least as extreme as ours if H₀ were true.

Two mistakes are possible. A type I error rejects H₀ when it is true (probability α). A type II error keeps H₀ when there really is an effect (probability β). Power = 1 − β is the chance of detecting a real effect — the green area under the blue curve.

Power grows with the size of the effect and with the sample size, and falls when you make α stricter. Small studies of small effects are mostly noise: even when the effect is real they usually “find nothing”. This matters for your own empirical work — “not significant” often just means “not enough data”.

Try this

  1. Set the effect to 0 and press Run one study twenty times. About one study in twenty is still “significant” at 5% — that's α.
  2. Choose Small study. The real effect is there, but power is low, so most single studies miss it.
  3. Now choose Big study. The blue curve moves far right and power gets close to 100%.
  4. Switch to α = 1%. Fewer false alarms, but power drops: the two errors trade off.

Common mistakes

  • Reading p = 0.03 as “3% chance that H₀ is true”. The p-value assumes H₀ is true; it says how surprising the data would be in that case.
  • Treating “not significant” as “no effect”. With low power, a real effect is easy to miss.
  • Treating “significant” as “important”. With a huge sample, a tiny, useless effect can be highly significant.
