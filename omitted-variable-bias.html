Omitted variable bias: correlation is not causation

A regression slope only measures a causal effect if nothing left out of the model drives both variables. Leave out such a variable and the estimate can be too big, too small, or even the wrong sign.

Change the inputs

Scenarios

The idea

Say you regress economic growth on defence spending (% of GDP) across countries and find a negative slope. Does defence spending hurt growth? Not necessarily. Countries facing a bigger security threat spend more on defence and may grow more slowly because of the uncertainty — think of the Baltic states next to Russia. The threat is a confounder.

If the threat is left out of the regression, the defence-spending coefficient picks up part of the threat's effect. This is omitted variable bias. Its size is the threat's effect on growth (γ) times how strongly the threat moves together with defence spending (δ): bias = γ · δ. Both must be non-zero for a bias to appear.

The fix is to control for the confounder: add it to the regression (multiple regression), or use panel data with fixed effects to remove things that don't change over time (next topic). When the confounder can't be measured, economists look for natural experiments or instrumental variables.

This is the central problem in the defence spending and growth literature: rich countries can afford more defence, threatened countries need more, and both of those also affect growth. A good term paper says which confounders it controls for and which it can't.

Common mistakes

  • Adding every variable you can find. Controls should be confounders (things that cause both x and y), not consequences of x — controlling for those can create new bias.
  • Thinking a high R² proves causality. A regression can fit well and still be badly biased.
  • Believing a non-significant result rules out an effect when an omitted variable could be pushing the estimate towards zero.

Key formulas

y = β·x + γ·z + u

True model: z is the confounder

b̂(short) ≈ β + γ·δ

Simple regression of y on x leaves out z

δ = cov(x, z) / var(x)

How z moves with x

Bias = γ · δ

Zero if z doesn't affect y or isn't related to x
