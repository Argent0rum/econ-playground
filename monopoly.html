Monopoly vs competition

A monopolist faces the whole downward-sloping demand curve, so selling one more unit means cutting the price on every unit. That changes its best choice.

Scenarios

The idea

A monopoly is a market with a single seller and no close substitutes, protected by barriers to entry: patents, control of a key resource, huge fixed costs, or government licences.

Unlike a competitive firm, a monopolist can't sell more without lowering its price — on all units. So its marginal revenue is below the price, and for a straight-line demand curve the MR line falls twice as steeply. The monopolist produces where MR = MC, then reads the price off the demand curve.

The result: a higher price and a lower quantity than a competitive market with the same costs. Some consumer surplus is transferred to the firm as profit — that's a redistribution. But some trades don't happen at all, even though buyers value them above their cost — that's the deadweight loss, a real loss to society.

That's why governments use competition law, regulate natural monopolies such as electricity grids and water networks, and sometimes prefer public ownership. Latvia's Competition Council (Konkurences padome) is the body that enforces this.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a monopolist charges the highest possible price. It charges the profit-maximising price, which balances price against quantity.
  • Setting price where MR = MC. MR = MC gives the quantity; the price comes from the demand curve above it.
