Price indices and real values

A price index sums up how the cost of a basket of goods changed. Which basket you use — this year's or the base year's — changes the answer, and so does your real wage.

The idea

A price index compares what a basket of goods costs now with what it cost in a base year (index = 100). Our basket has three goods a student household buys every month: bread, fuel and electricity, with base-year prices €1.40, €1.60 per litre and €0.18 per kWh.

The Laspeyres index keeps the base-year quantities: “what would last year's shopping cost today?” The Paasche index uses current quantities: “what would today's shopping have cost last year?” When a price shoots up, people buy less of it, so Laspeyres overstates the rise in the cost of living (it ignores substitution) and Paasche understates it. The Fisher index, the geometric mean of the two, sits in between.

Official consumer price indices (like the CSP's patēriņa cenu indekss) are Laspeyres-type indices whose weights are updated regularly. The real wage is the nominal wage divided by the price index: if wages rise 20% and prices 25%, workers can buy less than before.

The same logic gives real GDP, real interest rates and real defence spending (nominal spending divided by a deflator) — anything you compare across years should be in real terms.

Try this

  1. Choose Energy shock. Laspeyres rises the most, Paasche the least, and the real wage falls even though the nominal wage rises.
  2. Choose Everything +10%. With equal price rises and no change in quantities, all three indices agree.
  3. Choose No substitution. If people don't change what they buy, Laspeyres and Paasche become equal.
  4. Set the nominal wage change equal to the Fisher index rise. The real wage stays the same.

Common mistakes

  • Subtracting percentages instead of dividing: real change = (1 + wage growth) / (1 + inflation) − 1. Subtraction is only a rough shortcut for small numbers.
  • Mixing up which quantities each index uses. Laspeyres → base year (L for “last”), Paasche → current year.
  • Comparing nominal values across years. Always deflate first.
