Price elasticity of demand

Elasticity measures how strongly buyers react to a price change, in percentages. It decides whether raising your price brings in more money or less.

The idea

Price elasticity of demand is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. Because demand slopes down it's negative, so people usually quote the absolute value |e|.

If |e| > 1, demand is elastic: buyers react strongly. If |e| < 1, it's inelastic: buyers keep buying anyway. Necessities with no substitutes (medicine, fuel in the short run) tend to be inelastic; things with many substitutes (one brand of coffee) tend to be elastic. Time matters too — demand becomes more elastic as people find alternatives.

The surprising part: slope is not elasticity. This demand curve is a straight line with the same slope everywhere, yet elasticity changes along it. At the top, price is high and quantity small, so a €1 change is a small percentage of price but a big percentage of quantity — elastic. At the bottom it's the reverse.

That's why the revenue rectangle (price × quantity) is biggest in the middle, exactly where |e| = 1. Elasticity tells a firm which way to move its price, and tells a government which taxes will raise revenue without killing the market.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a steep curve “inelastic” everywhere. Steepness depends on units; elasticity is about percentages and changes along the curve.
  • Forgetting the sign. Most textbooks give price elasticity as negative; in exams check whether they want the absolute value.
