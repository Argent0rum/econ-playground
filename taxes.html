Taxes, who pays, and deadweight loss

A tax drives a wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers keep. Who actually carries it has nothing to do with who hands the money to the government — it depends on elasticities.

Scenarios

The idea

A tax of €t per unit means buyers pay one price and sellers keep a price €t lower. On the graph, it acts like shifting supply up by the tax (or demand down — the result is identical). The quantity traded falls to the point where the gap between the curves equals the tax.

The government collects the purple rectangle: tax × quantity still traded. But the market also loses the yellow triangle, the deadweight loss: trades that would have been worth more to buyers than they cost sellers, which no longer happen because of the tax. Nobody receives this value; it simply disappears.

Tax incidence is about who really pays. It doesn't matter whether the law says the seller or the buyer hands over the money; what matters is who is less able to change their behaviour. The side with the less elastic (steeper) curve carries more of the burden. That's why excise taxes on fuel, alcohol and tobacco mostly land on consumers.

Deadweight loss grows roughly with the square of the tax: doubling a tax about quadruples the loss. Many small taxes on broad bases cause less damage than one big tax on a narrow base.

Try this

  1. Click Inelastic demand. Buyers now carry most of the tax, and the deadweight loss is small because quantity barely falls.

  3. Set the tax to 20, note the deadweight loss, then set it to 40. The loss roughly quadruples.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming the side that legally pays the tax is the side that bears it.
  • Thinking tax revenue is the cost of the tax. The true cost to society is revenue plus the deadweight loss.
