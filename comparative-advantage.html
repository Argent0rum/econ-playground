Comparative advantage and trade

Even a country that's better at making everything gains from trade. What matters isn't who's best, but who gives up less to make each good.

The idea

A country has an absolute advantage if it can make more of a good with the same resources. It has a comparative advantage if it can make that good at a lower opportunity cost — giving up less of other things.

David Ricardo's insight (1817) was that comparative advantage, not absolute advantage, drives the gains from trade. Even if one country is better at everything, the two countries' opportunity costs almost always differ. Each should specialise in the good it gives up least to make, then trade.

The trade price has to lie between the two countries' opportunity costs. If cheese costs A 0.5 software and B 2 software, any price between 0.5 and 2 software per cheese makes both sides better off. Here the graph uses a price in between and shows each country's consumption before (hollow) and after trade (filled). After trade, both end up beyond what they could make alone.

The model leaves out a lot — transport costs, workers who lose their jobs in shrinking industries, changing productivity — which is why trade policy is still debated. But the core logic explains why small open economies like the Baltic states trade so much.

Common mistakes

  • Deciding specialisation by who produces more (absolute advantage) instead of who gives up less (comparative advantage).
  • Forgetting that the opportunity cost of one good is the inverse of the other: if 1 cheese costs 0.5 software, 1 software costs 2 cheese.
