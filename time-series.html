Time series: growth rates, trend and seasonality

A time series (dinamikas rinda) shows how a value changes over time. Shape a quarterly series and see how growth rates, the trend and the seasonal pattern are measured.

The idea

A time series lists values of the same indicator over equal time steps — here 4 years of quarterly data (Q1 2023 to Q4 2026). Statistics on time series answer three questions: how much did it change, how fast, and what is just the season?

Absolute increase is the difference in units: chain (versus the previous period) or base (versus the first period). The growth coefficient (augšanas temps) is the ratio yt/yt−1, and the growth rate (pieauguma temps) is that ratio minus 100%. Averages over the whole series use the first and last values: the average absolute increase is (yn − y1)/(n − 1), and the average growth coefficient is the geometric mean (yn/y1)1/(n−1).

Quarterly data usually has a seasonal pattern. A 4-quarter centred moving average averages over a whole year, so the seasons cancel out and the underlying direction shows. Dividing each value by the moving average and averaging by quarter gives seasonal indices: 120% means that quarter is typically 20% above the yearly level.

A linear trend ŷ = a + b·t is the least-squares line through the data; b is the average change per quarter. Comparing a quarter with the same quarter a year earlier (year-on-year) is the quick way to remove seasonality without any modelling.

Try this

  1. Choose Tourism. The chain growth rate jumps up and down every quarter, but the moving average and the year-on-year rate tell a calm story.
  2. Switch the moving average to 3-quarter: it no longer cancels a 4-quarter season, so the wiggles come back.
  3. Choose Noisy data and change the random sample. The trend line barely moves, single growth rates jump around.
  4. Choose Decline: growth rates turn negative and the growth coefficient drops below 100%.

Common mistakes

  • Averaging growth rates arithmetically. The average growth coefficient is a geometric mean of the chain coefficients, which equals (yn/y1)1/(n−1).
  • Mixing up augšanas temps (ratio, e.g. 104%) and pieauguma temps (change, e.g. +4%).
  • Comparing Q3 with Q2 for seasonal data and calling it growth. Use year-on-year or seasonally adjusted figures.
  • Using an even moving average without centring it — it then sits between two quarters.
