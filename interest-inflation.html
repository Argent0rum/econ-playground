Compound interest, inflation and real value

A savings account at 3% sounds safe, but if prices rise 4% a year you get poorer every year. This page shows the difference between how much money you have and what it can buy.

The idea

Compound interest means you earn interest on your interest. After t years, an amount grows to A × (1 + i)ᵗ. Small differences in the rate turn into big differences over decades, which is why starting to save early matters so much.

Inflation works the same way in reverse: if prices rise by π per year, €1 in t years buys what €1 / (1 + π)ᵗ buys today. The nominal value is the number of euros in your account; the real value is what those euros can actually buy.

The real interest rate is the gap between the two. The exact version is (1 + i) / (1 + π) − 1; the Fisher shortcut r ≈ i − π works well when rates are low. When inflation is above the interest rate, the real rate is negative and savers lose purchasing power — this happened to most euro savings accounts in 2022–2023, when inflation in the Baltics was among the highest in the euro area.

A handy rule: money doubles in about 70 ÷ (rate in %) years. At 7% that's about 10 years; at 2% it's 35.

Try this

  1. Click Savings account: the blue line rises, but the green real line falls below your starting amount.
  2. Set inflation to 0. The nominal and real lines become the same — inflation is the only thing separating them.
  3. Compare 5% and 7% returns over 40 years. The gap is far bigger than 2% sounds.

Common mistakes

  • Adding interest rates instead of compounding them: 10 years at 5% is +63%, not +50%.
  • Judging an investment by its nominal return without subtracting inflation.
