Money creation and the money multiplier

When a bank lends, it creates a new deposit somewhere else in the system. Follow the rounds of lending and see how far a single injection of central bank money can stretch.

The idea

The monetary base (MB) is the money the central bank creates directly: cash plus the reserves banks hold at the central bank. The money supply (M1, M2) is much larger, because most money is bank deposits — and banks create deposits when they lend.

Say €500 m of new central bank money ends up deposited in a bank. The bank must keep a share r as required reserves, may keep extra excess reserves e, and lends out the rest. The borrower spends it, the seller deposits it in another bank, and that bank lends again. Each round is smaller, and the total converges to a limit.

Two leaks shrink the multiplier: reserves that banks don't lend (r and e) and cash that people keep outside banks (c). With no cash and only required reserves, the multiplier is simply 1/r.

In practice the chain runs the other way round too: banks first decide to lend (creating a deposit), then find the reserves they need. The multiplier is still a good way to see what limits money creation — reserve rules, banks' caution and the public's demand for cash. In 2008–09, banks hoarded reserves, so huge increases in the monetary base barely moved the money supply.

Try this

  1. Start with Textbook: r = 10% gives a multiplier of 10, so €500 m becomes €5,000 m.
  2. Choose Eurozone today. The ECB's reserve requirement is only 1%, yet cash holdings and excess reserves keep the multiplier far below 100.
  3. Choose Crisis. Watch the curve flatten after a few rounds — that is why quantitative easing raised the base much more than the money supply.
  4. Raise c step by step and notice it lowers the multiplier even when reserve rules stay the same.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the money multiplier with the Keynesian (spending) multiplier. One multiplies the money stock, the other multiplies GDP.
  • Forgetting the cash leak: the full multiplier is (1 + c)/(r + e + c), not 1/r.
  • Thinking reserves are "lent out". Banks lend by creating deposits; reserves move between banks when the money is spent.
  • Mixing up M0/MB (base money) and M1/M2 (money supply).
